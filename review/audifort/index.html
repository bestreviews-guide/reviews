Does Audifort Work? What the Evidence Says

There's no published trial of Audifort itself, and no published trial showing this supplement — or any similar one — improves hearing or cures tinnitus. That's the honest starting point, and it shapes everything else in this review.

The ingredients do have some research behind them, in isolation and at higher doses. Green tea and grape seed are antioxidants, and oxidative stress is linked to age-related hearing damage. L-arginine has been studied for blood flow, and circulation to the inner ear matters for hearing. GABA has been tested in small trials for relaxation and sleep, which may explain why some buyers say they feel calmer and sleep better.

But those studies don't prove this blend works. The doses here are far smaller, the ingredients are mixed together, and hearing loss and tinnitus have many causes. Ginkgo has also been studied for tinnitus, with mixed results — but, as I said above, it isn't on this label, so it can't help here. The best evidence for tinnitus and hearing loss remains sound therapy and hearing aids, not a supplement.

What results are actually realistic? Some buyers say they notice a difference after the first week, and the official FAQ suggests best use over four months or longer. I wouldn't expect restored hearing. At most, I'd look for small changes in ear comfort, ringing, calm or sleep over weeks — and I'd be honest with myself if nothing changed. Note too that four months runs past the 90-day refund window, so you can't judge it for that long and still claim your money back.
