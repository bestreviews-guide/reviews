Does CircO2 Work? What the Evidence Says

Two halves again: the ingredients are well studied, the lozenge itself is not — at least not in any published trial I could find — and the headline claim can’t be checked against the label, because the doses are hidden.

The strongest ingredient evidence is beetroot. A 2015 randomized trial in Hypertension gave 64 patients with high blood pressure dietary nitrate (beetroot juice) for four weeks and saw systolic blood pressure fall by about 8 mmHg versus placebo (Kapil et al., 2015, PMID 25870193). L-citrulline has solid pharmacology too: a 2008 study showed oral L-citrulline raises blood arginine levels more effectively than L-arginine itself, because it bypasses breakdown in the liver (Schwedhelm et al., 2008, PMID 18254878). Hawthorn has a Cochrane review behind it for chronic heart failure, though the evidence there is modest and old (Pittler et al., 2008, PMID 18254076).

But here’s the gap. Those studies used full doses of single ingredients — grams of citrulline, real beetroot juice. CircO2’s 420 mg blend holds four ingredients, so whatever is in there is a fraction of the studied amounts. The page’s “200% in 20 minutes” figure, and blood flow up 34%, come from testing of the lozenge method itself, described on the page without a published paper to check. The MIT mouth-bacteria science is genuine, and dissolving a nitrate lozenge on the tongue is a sensible way to use it — but a sensible method with small doses is still a small dose.

So what’s realistic? In the first few days, usually nothing obvious. Some people report feeling a bit more energetic or breathing a little easier, which is plausible for a nitrate lozenge — but it’s also exactly what you’d expect from the placebo effect in week one. Weeks 2 to 4 are where the beetroot research shows blood pressure changes, if they’re going to happen. If you track your own blood pressure at home, this is the window to watch — same time of day, same arm, and compare against your own baseline, not the sales page’s stories. Months 2 to 3 are the page’s own recommended window, and they’re also the window the 90-day guarantee covers. One line belongs in every review of this product: there is no published, independent clinical trial of CircO2 itself.
