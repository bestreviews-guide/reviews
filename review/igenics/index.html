Does iGenics Work? What the Evidence Says

Straight answer: the ingredients have real research behind them. The product itself doesn't. Those are two very different things, and the sales page blurs them.

The strongest evidence is AREDS2 — the National Eye Institute trial published in JAMA in 2013, with thousands of people aged 50-plus. It found that lutein and zeaxanthin helped slow age-related vision decline, and they became the standard macular formula. iGenics contains that core at reasonable doses, which is a genuine plus. Then there's bilberry: a large randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study in 281 office workers found it improved vision and eye fatigue after 8 weeks. And a 2020 Yale study found ginkgo helped visual field indices in glaucoma patients.

But here's the catch. None of those studies tested iGenics. The page's own story — one woman's vision improving in 90 days, “new eyes in 7 days” — is anecdote and theory, not a trial. And the claim that iGenics “makes Lasik and lenses obsolete” is just wrong — no supplement replaces glasses or surgery. Judge it as a well-dosed nutrient formula, not a cure.
