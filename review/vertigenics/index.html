Does It Work? What the Evidence Actually Says

Here's the honest part, and it's the part that shaped my score. I couldn't find any clinical trial of Vertigenics itself, or of this exact combination, tested against vertigo, dizziness or balance. The ingredients are real and the doses are printed, but the story connecting them to vertigo hasn't been tested.

Balance is complicated, and that's worth understanding before you buy anything for it. It depends on your inner ear and vestibular system, your brainstem and cerebellum, what you see, and the position signals from your muscles and joints. Add in nerve health, blood sugar and oxidative stress, and you've got a lot of moving parts — which is probably why Vertigenics takes a broad, general-wellness approach instead of targeting one mechanism.

Looked at ingredient by ingredient, the picture is mixed. Zinc has decent evidence for colds, not for vertigo. Alpha lipoic acid has been studied for nerve health at around 600 mg a day, while this gives you 150 mg. Berberine has been studied at 900–1,500 mg a day, while this gives you 100 mg — and a small 2008 pilot study is a long way from proof. Green tea's EGCG and Korean ginseng both have research behind them for brain and metabolic health, but again, not for vertigo specifically. And resveratrol is a plausible antioxidant, not a vertigo remedy.

It's also worth noting what's missing. There's no ginkgo, no ginger, no vitamin D and no vinpocetine — ingredients that show up in a lot of balance and circulation formulas. So what you're getting is really a general antioxidant and blood-sugar-style blend, in modest doses, rather than a targeted vertigo formula. That doesn't make it useless, but it does mean you should keep your expectations realistic.
