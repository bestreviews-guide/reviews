Does Visiflora Work? What the Evidence Says

There's no trial of Visiflora itself, so I had to judge it on the research behind its ingredients — and that research is a mixed bag. The strongest evidence here is AREDS2, the National Eye Institute trial. The first AREDS trial, in 2001, and AREDS2, in 2013, showed that a high-dose antioxidant and zinc formula slowed the progression of macular degeneration in high-risk people, and that lutein and zeaxanthin could replace beta-carotene — which AREDS2 removed because it raised lung cancer risk in smokers.

Visiflora borrows that idea, but it doesn't copy the doses. Its zinc is 11 mg, against 25 mg in AREDS2, and it still contains beta-carotene, which AREDS2 dropped for smokers. The other ingredients are even harder to check: saffron has been studied at 20 mg a day, bilberry studies used far more than could fit in this blend, and ginkgo is usually studied at 120–240 mg a day for circulation and memory. With 14 ingredients sharing 351 mg, none of them can be at those levels — and the label doesn't tell you how much of any one you're actually getting.

The gut–eye story has the same problem. Gut microbiome changes have been reported in age-related macular degeneration, and probiotics and prebiotics have been studied for inflammation, but Visiflora contains neither, so that mechanism is speculation, not proof. On results, don't expect a noticeable change in the first few weeks. If anything changes, it's likely to be modest and gradual, after a month or more — and for a healthy eye, a supplement can't undo years of strain overnight.
