Does Yu Sleep Work? What the Evidence Says

Start with the ingredient that does have a clear dose. Melatonin has the best evidence of the lot. A 2013 meta-analysis by Ferracioli-Oda and colleagues (PMID: 23691095) found that melatonin shortened the time it took to fall asleep and improved overall sleep quality, with the strongest effects at low doses. That is exactly what Yu Sleep is selling, and it is a point in its favour. A low dose is also less likely to leave you groggy in the morning than the 5 mg or 10 mg doses that are common in gummies.

Magnesium has decent support too. A 2012 study by Abbasi and colleagues (PMID: 23853635) found that magnesium supplementation improved sleep time and sleep efficiency in older adults with insomnia. L-theanine also has a small but real signal: a 2019 study by Hidese and colleagues (PMID: 31623400) found it improved sleep quality and helped people feel more refreshed. The catch, as always, is the dose. Those studies used 300–500 mg of magnesium and 200 mg of L-theanine, and I have no way of knowing how much of either is actually in a serving of Yu Sleep.

So what should you realistically expect? Based on the label and the research, the most likely benefit is falling asleep a little faster and waking up less groggy than you would on a high-dose melatonin gummy. Deeper sleep, staying asleep all night and waking up truly refreshed are harder to promise, because the blend ingredients that are supposed to deliver them are under-dosed or unmeasured. Yu Sleep is a nudge, not a reset, and anyone expecting it to fix long-standing insomnia on its own is likely to be disappointed.
